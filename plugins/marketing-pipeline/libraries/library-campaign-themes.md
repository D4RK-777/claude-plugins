# Library: Campaign Themes
> Every campaign needs ONE declared strategic theme that steers all downstream creative, copy, and channel decisions. The theme is the emotional/psychological centre of gravity for the campaign — the "what story are we telling" answer. Consumed by `theme-selector` (selects ONE), `creative-strategy-selector` (picks an aligned strategy), `hook-creative-generator` (writes hooks that serve the theme), and `creative-interrogator` (audits whether work serves the declared theme).

---

## THE CORE TRUTH

> **A campaign without a declared theme is a campaign that argues with itself.**

Every ad, LP section, email, and retargeting message in a campaign should be a different expression of the SAME underlying argument. The theme is that argument. When the theme is missing or implicit, different assets pull in different directions — and the cumulative effect on the buyer is confusion, not motion.

A declared theme answers, in one sentence: *"This campaign is asking the buyer to feel X about Y, and to act because Z."* Every creative decision after that is just sharpening that sentence.

---

## HOW TO PICK A THEME

A theme is not a creative tactic. It is a strategic posture. Pick based on:
1. **The persona's pain stack and desire map** (icp-persona-engine Layers 2–3) — which layer is loudest?
2. **The character's dominant decision style** (icp-character-builder Layer 4) — which theme fires that style hardest?
3. **The sophistication stage** — themes that work at Stage 1 (plain claim) fail at Stage 5 (identity); the reverse is also true
4. **The campaign goal** — awareness, lead-gen, conversion, retention, win-back; certain themes are built for certain goals

The theme is locked AT THE BRAND BRIEF / EARLY-PIPELINE STAGE. Once declared, downstream skills serve it. Don't switch themes mid-campaign — that produces fragmentation.

## THE 12 STRATEGIC THEMES

Each theme has: a one-line description, the psychology that powers it, when to use it, when NOT to use it, and the creative tone that fits.

### 1. LOSS AVERSION

> "Show them what they're already losing by not having this."

- **Psychology:** Kahneman/Tversky — losses register psychologically about 2× more strongly than gains. Buyers will act faster to stop pain than to chase upside.
- **Best for:** Solution-Aware and Product-Aware buyers who know solutions exist but haven't pulled the trigger. Cost-of-inaction framing dominates.
- **Worst for:** Unaware buyers (they don't yet know there's a loss). Most-Aware buyers (they're past the pain frame and want the offer).
- **Tone:** Direct, specific, urgent without being alarmist. Quantify the loss if you can.
- **Example openers:**
  > "Most marketers waste 40% of their budget on ads they can't attribute. That's not a tech problem — that's a clarity problem."
  > "Every month you wait is another month of guessing what's working."

---

### 2. TRANSFORMATION / BEFORE-AFTER

> "Here is who they are now. Here is who they could be."

- **Psychology:** Identity arc. People don't buy products — they buy a better version of themselves. The "after" must be specific and visualisable.
- **Best for:** Outcome-driven categories (fitness, finance, B2B productivity, education). Aspirational buying motions.
- **Worst for:** Mechanism-heavy purchases where the buyer wants HOW, not WHAT. Risk-averse decision styles that need proof, not aspiration.
- **Tone:** Specific contrast. The "before" should be uncomfortably accurate — the "after" should be earned, not handed.
- **Example openers:**
  > "Six months ago Sarah was reviewing 14 dashboards every Monday. Today she has one — and her CMO finally stopped asking her where the leads were."

---

### 3. IDENTITY / TRIBE

> "This is what people LIKE YOU choose."

- **Psychology:** In-group signaling. People act faster when an action confirms membership in a group they want to belong to.
- **Best for:** Sophistication Stage 4–5 (the market is exhausted by claims; identity is what's left). Categories with strong cultural identity (creator tools, founder tools, design tools, luxury, niche consumer).
- **Worst for:** Unaware buyers (they don't yet know what tribe is being signalled). Heavily rational purchases where identity is decorative.
- **Tone:** Confident, slightly exclusionary. The point isn't "buy this," it's "you're already one of us, this is just the move."
- **Example openers:**
  > "Operators who actually know their CAC don't run their business on guesswork."
  > "Founders who ship don't ask permission to use better tools."

---

### 4. AUTHORITY / EXPERT POSITIONING

> "The people who really know say this."

- **Psychology:** Cialdini — authority transfer. Buyers offload cognitive load to credible experts.
- **Best for:** High-stakes purchases (B2B SaaS, enterprise, healthcare, finance). Risk-averse decision styles. Categories where the buyer is making the decision FOR someone else.
- **Worst for:** Casual/impulse categories. Identity-driven niches where expert endorsement reads as out-of-touch.
- **Tone:** Restrained, specific, sourced. Name the experts. Show the credentials.
- **Example openers:**
  > "The CFO of [recognisable name] cut their close cycle from 14 days to 6 using this approach. Here's what they did."

---

### 5. SOCIAL PROOF / MOMENTUM

> "Everyone is already moving — don't be the last."

- **Psychology:** Cialdini — social proof. Especially powerful when buyers are uncertain and want to defer to the group.
- **Best for:** Categories with network effects, marketplaces, tools that benefit from team adoption. Buyers in the "social-proof-driven" decision style.
- **Worst for:** Early-market products where there isn't enough proof yet. Identity-niches where the buyer specifically does NOT want to do what everyone else is doing.
- **Tone:** Specific numbers, named accounts, recent dates. "12,000 marketers in 2025" beats "join the community."
- **Example openers:**
  > "12,847 founders switched in the last 90 days. Here's the spreadsheet of why."

---

### 6. SCARCITY / URGENCY

> "This window is closing."

- **Psychology:** Loss aversion + reactance. People want things more when they perceive supply or time as constrained — but ONLY if the constraint is believable.
- **Best for:** Hot audiences (Most Aware), retargeting Stage 5, real launch windows, real cohort programs, real seasonal events.
- **Worst for:** Cold audiences (no relationship yet, urgency reads as desperation). Brands trying to build long-term trust — fabricated urgency erodes it permanently.
- **Tone:** Stated as fact. Specific date or count. Never theatrical.
- **Example openers:**
  > "12 seats. Cohort starts Sept 12. We don't open another until February."

---

### 7. NOVELTY / CATEGORICAL SHIFT

> "Everything you knew about this is now wrong."

- **Psychology:** Pattern interruption + curiosity gap. Strong opener for cold audiences because it bypasses category fatigue.
- **Best for:** Real innovation (new mechanism, new approach, new category). Sophistication Stage 3–4 (mechanism wins). Unaware → Problem-Aware audiences.
- **Worst for:** Me-too products. Buyers who've heard "revolutionary" so often the word triggers skepticism. Stage 5 markets where novelty claims are noise.
- **Tone:** Confident, specific about WHAT is new, willing to undermine the old way. Earn the claim with the mechanism.
- **Example openers:**
  > "Attribution doesn't work because cookies are dead. Server-side tracking changes the math."

---

### 8. FOMO (FEAR OF MISSING OUT)

> "Other people are getting results from this. You're not."

- **Psychology:** Social proof + loss aversion combined. Sharper edge than either alone.
- **Best for:** Categories where competitive standing matters (sales tools, growth tools, hiring tools). Buyers whose identity is tied to staying ahead.
- **Worst for:** Categories driven by craft or quality (FOMO undermines the brand). Risk-averse buyers who interpret FOMO framing as manipulation.
- **Tone:** Specific (not vague "everyone's doing X"). Name names. Show outcomes.
- **Example openers:**
  > "Your three biggest competitors started using this in Q2. You're still using v2."

---

### 9. WIN-BACK / RE-ENGAGEMENT

> "Something changed. Come back and see."

- **Psychology:** Pattern interrupt + sunk-cost reversal. Acknowledges the relationship lapsed and offers a reason to re-engage that doesn't blame the buyer.
- **Best for:** Lapsed customers, abandoned trial users, churned subscribers, dormant email lists.
- **Worst for:** Net-new acquisition. Cold audiences (there's nothing to win back).
- **Tone:** Warm, specific about what's changed, no guilt-tripping. Reset the relationship.
- **Example openers:**
  > "Last time you used [product], the attribution dashboard didn't exist. It does now."

---

### 10. STATUS / ASPIRATION

> "This is what the people you want to be like already use."

- **Psychology:** Veblen + social hierarchy. Buyers position themselves by what they own/use/wear/run-on.
- **Best for:** Premium positioning, design-led tools, luxury, B2B tools where reputation matters internally (the buyer wants to look smart for choosing this).
- **Worst for:** Value-led categories. Mass-market products where exclusivity feels exclusionary, not aspirational.
- **Tone:** Restrained. Show the use, not the claim. Let the buyer recognize the signal.
- **Example openers:**
  > "Quietly used by the marketing teams at [recognisable names]. We don't promote it. They don't either."

---

### 11. BELONGING / COMMUNITY

> "There are people who get it. Come join them."

- **Psychology:** Social belonging is a core human need. Tools and brands that offer membership in a community outperform tools that sell features.
- **Best for:** Categories with strong community gravity (creator tools, fitness, learning, niche software). Buyers whose decision style is identity-driven or social-proof-driven.
- **Worst for:** Categories where the buyer doesn't want a community (utility tools, infrastructure). Solo-buyer purchases.
- **Tone:** Warm, inclusive, specific about WHO the community is.
- **Example openers:**
  > "A group of 2,400 marketing leaders meet every Tuesday to compare what's actually working. The tool is part of it."

---

### 12. CONTRARIAN / NAMING WHAT NOBODY SAYS

> "The thing everyone knows but nobody says — we'll say it."

- **Psychology:** Pattern interrupt via taboo. Brands that name uncomfortable truths win disproportionate attention and trust because they signal honesty.
- **Best for:** Saturated categories (Stage 4–5) where everyone sounds the same. Brands with a strong founder voice. Categories where buyer cynicism is high.
- **Worst for:** New categories that haven't earned the right to be contrarian. Brands that can't actually back up the contrarian claim.
- **Tone:** Direct, slightly uncomfortable, never edgy-for-edgy's-sake. The contrarian point must be TRUE.
- **Example openers:**
  > "Most marketing reporting is theatre. We built ours to be useful instead."

## THE DECLARATION FORMAT (used by theme-selector)

```
DECLARED THEME: [name]
ONE-LINE ARGUMENT: "This campaign is asking [persona] to feel [emotional posture] about [topic] and to act because [reason]."
RATIONALE: [2-3 sentences — why THIS theme for THIS persona × THIS sophistication stage × THIS goal]
SECONDARY THEME (optional): [a complementary theme to lean on for warmer audiences or specific touchpoints]
EXCLUSIONS: [themes explicitly ruled OUT for this campaign and why — protects against drift]
```

## DOWNSTREAM SKILL INTEGRATION

This library is consumed by:
- **theme-selector** — selects ONE theme + rationale; logs to campaign-state Decision Log
- **creative-strategy-selector** — uses the declared theme to constrain strategy options (some strategies don't fit some themes)
- **hook-creative-generator** — every hook must serve the declared theme
- **lp-copy-generator** — LP scrolls progress the theme's argument
- **email-sequence-from-character** — each email is a chapter of the theme's argument
- **retargeting-cascade** — stages can layer in a secondary theme for warm/hot audiences
- **creative-interrogator** — audit phase verifies the theme is served, not betrayed

---

> **First principle:** A theme is not a creative tactic. It is the strategic spine of the entire campaign. Pick one. Defend it. Honour it everywhere. The campaigns that work are the ones where every asset is a different sentence in the same paragraph.
